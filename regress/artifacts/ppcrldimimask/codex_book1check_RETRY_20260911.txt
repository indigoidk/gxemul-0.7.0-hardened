2. **A computes exactly that mask for all 4,096 pairs.**

   Let:

   ```text
   E = 63 - SH                  inclusive endpoint
   T = (64 - SH) & 63           position immediately after E
   ```

   The body processes `m`, advances it modulo 64, and stops when the *next* position equals `T`. It therefore processes precisely the inclusive cyclic interval from `MB` through `E`.

   Its iteration count is:

   ```text
   L = 1 + ((E - MB) mod 64)
   ```

   Here `mod` means the nonnegative remainder. Consequently, **every execution takes between 1 and 64 iterations**.

   For `SH=0`, `E=63` and `T=0`. Starting at `MB=0`, the `do/while` processes positions `0,1,…,63`, then stops after advancing back to 0. The initial equality between `m` and `T` does not skip the body or cause another traversal. For other `MB`, it processes `MB,…,63`.

   For a wrapping example, `MB=63, SH=63` selects positions `63,0`, producing `0x8000000000000001`. A wrapping interval can also legitimately select every bit: `MB=1, SH=63` visits `1,…,63,0` exactly once each.

   **There is no nonterminating pair and no position processed twice.** In a full traversal, the final control value returns to the starting position, but the body does not execute there again.

   I checked all 4,096 pairs in an independent in-memory model against a mask defined by architectural bit membership. Results: zero mask mismatches, zero duplicate visits, zero nonterminations, and iteration counts from 1 through 64.

   The split-field decoding also matches Book I. Rotating with the separate counter `n` preserves `sh` for mask construction, addressing the settled counter-clobbering problem. The unsigned 64-bit rotation and final masked merge are correct, including when `RS=RA`, because `s` captures the original source before destination modification.

4. **B produces the correct result in all four compilation-width cases.**

   Define `μ` as the low 32 bits of architectural mask `m`, and let:

   ```text
   W  = ordinary 32-bit rotate-left of the original source word
   L′ = (old destination low word & ~μ) | (W & μ)
   H  = old destination high word
   ```

   The required results are:

   | Mask case | Compilation width | Required destination | B |
   |---|---:|---|---|
   | `MB <= ME` | 32 bits | `L′` | Correct |
   | `MB > ME` | 32 bits | `L′` | Correct |
   | `MB <= ME` | 64 bits | `H || L′` | Correct |
   | `MB > ME` | 64 bits | `W || L′` | Correct |

   **The conditional is correctly placed before the loop.** It tests the original mask endpoints. On completion, the loop has changed `mb` to `me`, so moving that test after the loop would make `mb > me` false.

   In the 64-bit compilation, the conditional preserves the low word and replaces the high word with `W`. Every subsequent loop mask is confined to the low word, so the loop preserves that prepared high word.

   **In the 32-bit compilation, the conditional assignment is an exact no-op.** Its intermediate expression is evaluated at a width sufficient to hold the shifted word, but assignment back to unsigned `uint32_t` discards those upper 32 bits. The remaining low word is precisely the old `ra`. The shift is safe because the cast to `uint64_t` happens before `<< 32`.

   The low-word loop inserts the selected rotated bits and preserves every unselected destination bit. The conditional rotation expression also avoids evaluating a shift by 32 when `SH=0`.

   An in-memory model using C-style unsigned truncation checked all **32,768 SH/MB/ME combinations**, with six source/destination patterns at each width: **196,608 cases per width, zero mismatches**. Every 32-bit case also matched execution with the conditional assignment omitted. The algebra above establishes the result for arbitrary register contents; the pattern checks supplement it.

   This establishes the 32-bit result at the stated register interface. A 32-bit compilation should not automatically be equated with a 64-bit processor executing in architectural 32-bit mode: the latter still has defined 64-bit register operations.
